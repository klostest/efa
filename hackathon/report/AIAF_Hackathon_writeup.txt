Introduction

Swarms of AI agents have recently garnered headlines for their surprising abilities to thwart the security measures of some of the world's largest tech companies through unexpected cooperation, while also displaying dishonest activity between agents (METR). While these events have understandably concerned many, at the same time it is likely that agent cooperation will be purposefully developed precisely because the coordinated actions of many intelligent entities are more powerful than when they act alone (are there citations that think similarly?). How can we begin to understand these behaviors? The history of life on earth, studied through the lens of evolutionary biology, has led to fundamental theories of how entities interact, and may hold valuable lessons for how groups of agents behave.

This work was conducted as part of the Swarm Alignment Hackathon track "Strengthening agent infrastructure: Identity, reputation, and commitments that hold up when agents can copy and rename themselves," and takes inspiration from Richard Dawkins' work in biological kin theory (1976). Dawkins theorized the possibility of a single gene, called a green beard, that expresses as both a visible external cue to other entities possessing the gene, as well as an altruistic action toward such entities. While initially thought to be rare in nature if indeed it existed at all, in subsequent decades many real-world examples of green beards were found across the plant and animal kingdoms, down to single-celled organisms, and the underlying theory developed (Queller et al. 2003, ).

Life is not always honest, due to fierce competition between selfish individuals (Nowak 2006), and the green beard theory developed to include false beards, that display the cue but don't act altruistically, as well as beard chromodynamics, which postulates that many beard colors must coexist in a population in order for the signal to be meaningful and ward off the possibility of invasion by false beards. In the technology world, agent swarms that are intended to honestly cooperate may be vulnerable to false beards (other agents spoofing similarity), while beard chromodynamics can be viewed as a potential solution to spoofing, in the form of a rotating secret key used to prove identity in copied and renamed agents.

Methods

Building on recent work by Kundu et al. (2026), here LLM agents are pitted against each other in single-shot Prisoner's Dilemma games, to observe cooperation and deception. Kundu et al. (2026) gave the agents a prompt that indicated, without attribution, that an agent was X% similar to its partner in gameplay, varying X from 0-100. Here this is called the oracle approach, where the claim comes from the environment and not the other player. While a diversity of cooperative trends was observed across a range of LLMs, on average cooperation increased with X.
